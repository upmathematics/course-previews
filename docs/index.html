The big picture

Algorithms and statistics now help decide who gets hired, who gets into college, who gets stopped by police, and how long someone is sentenced. This course looks at those systems through a quantitative lens. Used without transparency, these tools can hide and amplify discrimination. Used with transparency and oversight, they can be levers for equitable change. You’ll learn how data about people are collected and used, how to evaluate the way statistics are presented, and how to use mathematical evidence to question whether a system treats people fairly.

Questions the course takes up

  • What data are collected about you, and how do algorithms use them?
  • How can a statistic be accurate and still mislead?
  • What would make a risk score used in policing or sentencing fair?
  • How can admissions and hiring formulas create or reduce inequity?

The big picture

Real data are messy, and a single sample never tells the whole story. In Applied Statistics I, you’ll learn how to draw conclusions from a sample and how to measure how confident you can be in them. You’ll start with how a study is designed, since that decides what it can show. Then you’ll see why a statistic changes from sample to sample and how that variability becomes a confidence interval or a test of significance. From there you’ll compare two groups with t-procedures, compare several with one-way ANOVA, model a relationship with simple linear regression, and use simulation (randomization and the bootstrap) to see the logic behind it all. Programming in a statistical software package runs through the whole course, and the methods carry over to the sciences, health, business, and social sciences.

Questions the course takes up

  • Did the treatment cause the change, or could chance explain it?
  • How much can one sample tell us about a whole population?
  • When is a difference between groups large enough to take seriously?
  • How strong is the relationship between two variables, and how sure are we?

Tip: MTH 361 also meets the prerequisite for MTH 391.

The big picture

Real data rarely arrive ready to analyze. Fundamentals of Data Science follows the whole life cycle: importing and tidying messy data, reshaping and transforming it, visualizing and modeling it, and communicating what you found to the people who need to act on it. You’ll work with structured and unstructured data, including tables, text, time series, maps, and networks, writing readable code in both R and Python and building reproducible reports. Throughout, you’ll also ask whether the data collected is necessary for its purpose. A set of mini-projects throughout the semester gives you practice with each step, each one taking a data set from a first question to a clear finding.

Questions the course takes up

  • What question can this data actually answer, and what should we not ask of it?
  • How do you turn a messy file into a table you can analyze?
  • Which visualization makes a pattern clear instead of hiding it?
  • How do you explain a data-driven finding to someone who will act on it?

Note. Every figure and Explore More example on this page uses simulated (computer-generated) data. Simulation is a standard tool in statistics and data science: when you build the data yourself, you know the right answer, so you can check whether a method finds it, study one idea at a time, and avoid exposing anyone’s private information. The simulated numbers aren’t real-world findings. In the courses themselves, you’ll work with real data sets. Each Explore More panel explains why its example is simulated and shows how its data was made.
